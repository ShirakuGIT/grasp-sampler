Constrained shelf retrieval and transfer¶

The default MuJoCo demonstration uses a single FR3v2 and a two-tier cabinet. The default --suite expanded includes a Ritz box, mug, cup, plate, bowl, curved mustard bottle and blue vase, preserving original mesh colors. The box, cup and vase move from the lower tier to separate upper-tier slots. Mug, plate and bottle move to the tabletop, while the bowl uses an oblique rim grasp to move across the table. --suite household retains the earlier four-object demonstration. --suite classic selects the earlier mustard-bottle, can and foam-brick sequence. The lower bay retains 28 cm tier spacing; the expanded scene has a taller 40 cm upper bay for the bottle. Approach clearance still matters. Its purpose is to make grasp generation, rejection, selection, physical closure, and extraction visible. This is an illustrative scene inspired by the references below, not a reproduction of their benchmarks or a claim of comparable performance.

Fresh antipodal candidates, collision screening and physical finger contacts. Playback is 2×; cyan shows candidates, coral shows rejections and gold marks the selected grasp. Earlier recording on YouTube.

The scenes use original textured workspace scans in assets/household/. YCB objects retain source UV coordinates and texture pixels. The Ritz box retains the existing decimated geometry, with original dense reconstruction vertex colors baked onto a UV atlas. No object palette overrides these appearances. See asset attribution and preparation.

Mug, cup and plate contact geometry consists of separate CoACD convex pieces, leaving the handle opening and vessel cavities unfilled. Grasp sampling uses their actual scanned surfaces, including a handle-region filter for the mug and a rim filter with vertical finger closure for the plate. Box and classic objects use convex hull contact geometry. These decompositions approximate the scans; demo masses are 60 g (30 g for the lightweight plate) and do not claim YCB inertia fidelity. The household gripper has a 16 N tendon preload and higher closing stiffness, within the native +/-40 N actuator limit, to hold thin handle and rim contacts. Household pads use six-dimensional frictional contact, with 8 mm rolling-resistance length representing their finite 17 mm rubber contact surface. The central pad proxy extends 1.5 mm toward the midline so the pads meet at zero opening, removing the stock proxy’s 3 mm dead gap for thin rims. Placement remeasures the object after transit and uses pose feedback during descent to account for settling within the finger pads.

Mesh normalization and world poses stay consistent. Frontal and tilted world approach facets are transformed into the current object frame, including after physical settling/tipping. Candidate generation is not restricted to a vertical approach.

The sequence approaches, closes, lifts slightly to unseat the object, extracts through the cabinet opening, then lifts outside the shelf. The box is inserted into the upper tier; vessels and the plate turn around the vertical axis to approach the table edge while preserving their pickup orientation relative to gravity. Rim release uses a small opening before retracting, keeping the lower finger above the table. The classic suite reorients its table objects from the side. No object attachment constraints or object teleportation are used. Joint IK and contact screening use a scratch MuJoCo data instance; the live world is advanced with native actuators and frictional contacts. The box’s candidate screening checks upper-tier IK and collisions as well as pickup feasibility. Household screening also checks extraction/lift IK, collisions with the carried geometry at lift, and table placement IK over several yaw angles. The approach reuses its screened IK solution. Selection combines joint travel and contact-centering costs, with a preference for horizontal rim insertion on the plate. These costs describe selection heuristics, not grasp probabilities.

Free-space joint moves are collision checked against the complete MuJoCo scene, including the held object’s measured tool-relative geometry. A direct joint path is used when clear; otherwise a small bidirectional RRT-Connect planner finds a route and collision-checks its shortcuts. All of this happens in scratch data: the live object is carried by contact, not by a weld or state reset. Joint edges are checked at a maximum 0.04-radian spacing; this is discrete collision checking, not a continuous swept-volume guarantee. The planner adapters can replace this demonstration controller with an application’s production motion planner.

Cyan TCP glyphs are sampled candidates, coral glyphs are rejected, and gold highlights the selected command and staging targets. The viewer has no text HUD or cost chart. Phase updates appear in the terminal, and feasible selection costs and rejection reasons are saved in the JSON report. Those costs are not robustness probabilities. The robot’s full source visual material regions preserve the white shell, dark accents and markings; native Menagerie dynamics are retained.

pip install -e ".[mujoco,fr3]"
python tools/prepare_fr3_mujoco.py
# macOS: use mjpython for the native viewer
PYTHONPATH=src mjpython examples/pick_place_mujoco.py --gui --hold
# Linux: python examples/pick_place_mujoco.py --gui --hold

On macOS the example selects the trimesh collision backend via GRASP_SAMPLER_DISABLE_OPEN3D=1 to avoid Open3D/MuJoCo’s duplicate GLFW classes. The default backend for other callers is unchanged.

PYTHONPATH=src python examples/pick_place_mujoco.py --headless \
    --report /tmp/shelf-picks.json --snapshot /tmp/shelf.png

The household and classic MuJoCo runs check force-bearing bilateral finger contact and a lift of at least 8 cm above the initial object height. Placement requires load-bearing contact on the requested tier/table, separation from the gripper, low settling velocity, a bottom height within 15 mm of the surface, and less than 4 cm XY error in both suites. Reports expose the actual error and the tolerance used. Placement orientation is free: a bottle settled on its side can pass, while the same bottle on the wrong tier cannot. Candidate IDs, rejection reasons, approach obliquity, selection costs and support forces are recorded. This is a single seeded demonstration, not a multi-seed benchmark success-rate estimate.

Playback performance¶

The native viewer defaults to --speed 2 --fps 60. Playback pacing and redraws are independent, and rendering is limited by wall time rather than every fixed number of physics steps. Sleep is batched while honoring the next frame deadline. Frame deadlines include synchronization work rather than adding a full interval afterward. Static glyphs are cached, and viewer synchronization sends integration state instead of repeatedly copying the complete visual model. Physics remains MuJoCo’s compiled CPU solver at a 2 ms timestep; faster playback does not skip contact integration or speed up motor commands in simulation time. Candidate-display pauses refresh only the viewer; they do not advance physics. This keeps live grasp sampling consistent with the headless validation sequence. The requested frame rate is a target, not a hardware-independent guarantee. JAX/MJX is useful for batched simulation workloads; this single native viewer uses the existing CPU solver and does not introduce a JAX dependency.

The expanded seven-object physical run completed all seven transfers, including three lower-to-upper shelf placements. Maximum XY placement error was 3.82 cm against the unchanged 4 cm tolerance. The run requires bilateral finger forces, an 8 cm lift, released and settled load-bearing contact on the requested support, and the correct object-bottom height. The recording above shows the full scene and transfers.

A local M4 / Retina timing check measured 60.0 scene updates per second, with a 95th-percentile update interval of 16.94 ms, after removing bitmap-text HUDs, disabling the table mirror pass, and using 2048-pixel shadows. The earlier loop measured 27.1 updates/s. These measurements describe scene-state synchronization, not physical display presentation; performance depends on the machine and workload.

Arm mounting and curved vase¶

The arm sits on a 240 × 240 × 12 mm aluminum plate with chamfered corners, beveled edges, four 22 mm counterbores and 9 mm through holes. The counterbore shoulders support the washers and recessed cap screws. A 23 mm pedestal and registration ring keep the original robot base height. This is illustrative mounting hardware, rather than a manufacturer-certified plate design. It is fixed visual geometry, as was the previous mount; robot dynamics are unchanged.

The blue vase has a bulbous body and narrow neck. Its original image and UVs are retained, with 29 convex contact pieces. Sampling uses the neck’s actual surface, and a longer extraction clears the wide body before lifting. Its lower-to-upper transfer passed with a 6 mm XY placement error in the complete seven-object physical run. Like the other example objects, its mass and inertia are illustrative rather than measured hardware parameters.
